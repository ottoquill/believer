## Experiments

He stood at the bathroom mirror at six in the morning and tried to tell his own face a lie.

The face was a good one for the work — he had been told so by people who meant it as a compliment and by people who meant it as a diagnosis, and the two camps were not always distinguishable. Grey at the temples, even-featured, built for a sincerity he had never had to feel. He studied it now the way he studied a client before a deposition, assessing what it could be made to do, and he said, quietly, to the glass, so as not to wake the house:

"My name is David."

It was a stupid lie, chosen for its stupidity, a lie with no stakes and no victim, the sort of thing a man says to test a microphone. *Check, check.* And the sting came anyway, prompt and small and exact, a brightness behind the eyes like the afterimage of a flashbulb, and under the tongue the thin metallic wrongness he had no better word for than the taste of a fork against a filling. It was nothing. It was less than a headache. But it had arrived in the same instant as the word — not after, not as a consequence he could have argued with, but *with* it, fused to it, the way heat is fused to flame and not produced by it — and Daniel gripped the edge of the sink and breathed and waited for it to recede, which it did, leaving the faint chemical taste and the conviction, which he had been resisting for thirty hours, that the thing was real and was his and was not going to stop.

He had spent yesterday telling himself it was a virus. A stress event. A man under load who had said something in his kitchen that he hadn't meant to say, and felt, when he tried to take it back, a specific lancing he was now feeling at the mirror, and had decided, because deciding was his gift, that it was a coincidence of nerves. Nadia had said something in that kitchen too. He was not ready to think about what Nadia had said. He had filed it the way he filed things, in the place he kept what he did not intend to look at, and he had gone to work and come home and lain awake beside her in a silence that had its own grain, and now it was morning and he was lying to a mirror to see if the thing would do it again.

It did it again. He said, "I slept well," and got the sting. He said, "I am calm," and got the sting — no worse than the first, though the second lie carried more of him in it. He stood with that. Both were small lies, lies with no one on the far end of them, no one to wound; both cost the small coin. So the price was not paid in the bigness of the claim. It was paid in the stakes, in who stood downwind of the word — and a lie told to no one but a mirror had no downwind at all, only him. He filed this, because terror and method had always shared a room in him, and he did not yet know how to weigh a lie with a person inside it, only that the scale existed and that he would have to learn to read it before it taught him the hard way.

He turned on the shower for the noise and sat on the closed lid of the toilet in his robe with his second phone — the clients-who-don't-exist phone — and he ran the first real experiment of his new life, which was to find out whether the thing followed him onto the line.

---

He called the firm's after-hours service, a recording, a woman's voice that had been a real woman's once and was now a permanent fixture, and when the beep came he said, into the dead channel, "This is Daniel Mercer and I am calling to report that I am a sea captain."

Nothing.

He said it again, louder, daring it — *I have never set foot on land, my mother was a herring* — and the only thing that happened was that the dead air received the words and did nothing back, no sting, no flashbulb, no foil, the clean painless nothing of a lie told into a wire. He had said worse than herrings, far worse, into far better phones, for twenty years; he had built a fortune on the principle that what you said into a microphone and what you said in a room were two different physics, and he had never once had reason to suspect they were two different physics in the literal sense — that there was a law about it, and that the law had found him, and that the law left open a door the exact shape of a telephone.

He tested it three more ways, because three was the number that turned an anecdote into a finding. He left himself a voice memo full of lies and felt nothing. He typed a paragraph of lies into the notes app — *I am eight feet tall; I have always been faithful; the Halcombe part passed every test on the books* — and felt nothing, not even at the last one, which he had put in to see. The clean result there told him only that he was safe in writing, or safe because he believed it; he could not yet separate the two, and he set the question aside as premature. Then he stood, turned off the shower he had not used, and went to the bedroom doorway where Nadia lay with her back to the door and her eyes open — he could tell by the breathing; he had been married eleven years — and said, to the room, to the back of her head, "I'm going in early."

The sting came. Small, but it came, and it came in a room with another person in it, and it had not come on the phone in a room with no one, and Daniel stood in the doorway with the taste of foil under his tongue and understood that he had just learned something enormous and that he did not understand it at all.

---

He did not go in early. He went to a coffee place forty minutes from the apartment, one with no chance of a colleague in it, and sat in the back with his coat on and watched people, which was the other half of the experiment, the half he had not admitted to himself he was running until he was already running it.

Because here was the thing he had felt in the doorway and needed to disprove. The phone had been safe and the room had not, and a sane, ordered universe would have made the difference about *him* — his mouth, his words, a curse on Daniel Mercer that traveled with Daniel Mercer's voice. But the doorway hadn't been about his voice. He'd had a thought there, fast and irrational, that he had dismissed and could not dismiss, and the coffee place was where he came to disprove it, and over an hour and three coffees he failed to disprove it.

A man at the next table was on the phone, arguing, and said, "No, I'm leaving now, I'm literally in the car," sitting in a chair holding a croissant, and felt nothing — Daniel watched his face the way he watched a monitor, and there was nothing in it, the easy fluency of a man lying into a wire from eight feet away. So the field, if it was a field, did not reach the phone even when the phone was near. That was consistent. The door held. Fine.

But then the barista, a girl of twenty with a ring through her septum, leaned across the counter to a regular — Daniel was perhaps fifteen feet off — and said, "Oh my god, I love that on you," about a coat that was, by any standard, a crime, and stopped. Mid-sentence. A small hitch, a wince so fast that anyone not in the business of reading faces would have missed it, her hand going to her temple for half a second before she laughed it off and rang up the order. The regular noticed nothing. Daniel noticed everything. He noticed because he had felt the same wince in his own face at the mirror an hour before, the flashbulb, the foil, the bright nothing-much of a trivial lie — and he had not said a word, and he was fifteen feet away.

He moved tables, sat by the window, twenty-five feet at least from the counter, and waited, and over the next half hour he watched the thing hold and not hold, the way a tide does at the line where it can't decide. People at the counter winced; people by the door did not. A woman near him told her child, "we'll go to the park later, I promise," and then came the flinch, the hand to the brow, the promise she had no intention of keeping landing on her like a thing she'd touched on a stove. By the time the third coffee was cold Daniel had stopped pretending he was disproving anything. He had taken a napkin and on it, in the small clean capitals he used for things he did not want anyone else to read, drawn a circle, and put a dot in the center, and the dot was him.

It was not his voice. It was not a curse on his mouth that traveled with his words. It was a thing he carried in his vicinity like a temperature, and everyone who came inside it fell under whatever law he had broken in his kitchen. He had walked into a coffee shop and made a dozen strangers, for the length of one croissant order, unable to tell each other their small kindnesses, and not one of them knew why, and not one of them knew it was him, and he had not said a single word.

He put a hand to his own temple, where the barista had put hers, and found no pain there. He had told no lie. He was, for the moment, the only safe man in the room — the only one who could lie freely, because he hadn't, and because lying took knowing, and right now he knew only that he was afraid and that the fear, said aloud, would have cost him. He left a large tip. He did not say *keep the change,* because he could not be sure, in that instant, that he wouldn't have wanted it back.

---

Over the next days he was thorough, because thoroughness was the last thing he had that resembled control, and because a man who has lost the ground under him will measure the air.

He learned the range by walking it. He learned that the edge was soft — that at the far wall of a large room the lies came only as a vague unease, people frowning at nothing, drifting without deciding toward the door, toward the windows, toward the open air where the law thinned out. He sat in the dead center of a hotel lobby on a hard chair and watched the room arrange itself around him over twenty minutes, the bellmen finding reasons to be at the doors, the concierge taking her calls a careful distance off, two businessmen with a deal to lie their way through carrying their drinks, unconsciously, to the far banquette. He sat in the cleared center like the eye of something, and the thought arrived whole: that he was now the empty middle of every room he entered, that people would feel it before they saw him and move to his edge without knowing they were moving, and that the centers of rooms would all be his, and he would have them to himself.

He learned that walls helped. At a coworking space he'd rented a desk in under a name that was almost his, he stood on one side of a glass partition and, through the open door, felt a stranger's small lies sting him, and with the door shut felt nothing, and he slid the door open and closed, open and closed, a child with a light switch, watching a man twenty feet away wince and not wince, and felt sick, and stopped.

He learned that silence was free and that silence was not safe, and he came to it sideways, when his assistant — his former assistant, he supposed, though no one had said the word — called the second phone to ask why he hadn't come in. He opened his mouth to say *everything's fine, family thing,* and could not — not because the phone would have stung him, it wouldn't have, but because a woman two feet from the microwave was waiting for her tea and would hear it, and would, if it were false, not be hurt by his lie but *see* it, the way this thing turned a room into a place where the only honest move was to say nothing and where saying nothing was its own loud confession. So he said, into the phone, in front of the woman, "I can't talk right now," which was true, and which told her everything. It was the first time he understood that the law had not abolished lying. It had taken the lie out of the mouth and put it into the gap between the words — into the pause, into the thing you declined to say — and a man who had spent his life in that gap was going to find that it knew exactly where he lived.

---

By the end of the week he had a system, and the system frightened him more than the symptoms had, because a system meant he had accepted the premise.

He took calls in hallways and stairwells now, alone, where the line was free and no body could read him. He wrote where he would once have spoken — texts, emails, a note slid under a door — because the page never stung and the page never told. He had spent twenty years teaching powerful men that the camera was the enemy and the room the refuge: that you said the real thing in the room and the safe thing to the lens. He had had it backwards, or the world had reversed under him. Now the room was the enemy and the screen the refuge, the phone the last private place left in which to be a human being, which was to say to occasionally, mercifully, not be true.

He calibrated the pain too, because he was a man who needed a scale, and because not knowing how far it went was worse than any single sting. He could not bring himself to run the high end on purpose. In the kitchen, when he had tried to take back the thing about Nadia, he had felt something past the foil and the flashbulb — a real surge, sweat, the room tilting, his mouth gone dry — and he had not gone near it since, the way you don't put your hand back on the stove to confirm the stove is hot. The small lies cost small change: foil, flashbulb, the bright nothing. The ones with a person on the other end and something at stake — he had brushed two by accident, and both times the change was larger, a band of pressure across the brow, a dampness at the neck. He did not test below that. He had a sense, animal and unreasoned, that the floor went a long way down, that there was a depth at which the stone he kept dropping would, this once, strike something, and that he did not want to be standing on it when it did.

---

He learned the last of it in an elevator, from a stranger, which was right, he thought afterward, since strangers were the only people left who would tell him anything.

He was going down. The coworking building had a slow car, and at the eleventh floor a man got in — sixties, good coat, the broad soft face of someone who sold things to people who could afford them — finishing a call as the doors closed. "Absolutely, you have my word, the unit's never been in a flood zone," he said into it, easy and warm, and felt nothing, fluent, free, the phone keeping him clean, and he pocketed it. The doors were shut now and the car was small and they stood three feet apart in a steel box, and a younger man in the corner muttered something to no one, and the salesman, out of the reflex of a man who fills silences, turned and said, "Cold out there, huh," and stopped.

Daniel watched it take him. He knew it before the man did — saw the wince start, the hand come halfway up to the temple and not finish, the confusion crossing the broad soft face, because it was not cold out, it was unseasonable, everyone had been remarking on it; the man had said the automatic untrue thing people say to fill an elevator, and the box held no phone, only Daniel, only the dot at the center, and the small lie about the weather had landed on him like a coal he could not account for.

The man's eyes watered. He looked at the lit numbers as if they had betrayed him. And then — this was the part Daniel would carry, the part that ended the experiments and began everything after — he turned, not to Daniel, he didn't know it was Daniel, he didn't know it was anyone; he turned to the younger man in the corner, a total stranger, and out of the pure animal need to discharge the pressure, to say one true thing and lift the inexplicable weight, said:

"It isn't cold. I don't know why I said that. It's the warmest December I can remember, and my wife is leaving me, and I haven't told a soul."

The car reached the lobby. The doors opened on bright marble and the warm wrong air. The salesman stood blinking, appalled at himself, his secret lying on the floor between three men who would never see one another again, and the younger man fled, and the salesman fled after him with his hand pressed to his mouth.

Daniel did not move. He stood in the open car with his finger not quite reaching the button, and understood at last and completely what he was. Not a man who could not lie. A field with a man at its center. Wherever he went he would open the floor of the elevator and the floor of the lobby and the floor of his own house, and the things underneath would come up whether anyone wanted them to or not, and no one would know it was him, and he would know it was him, every time, for the rest of his life.

The doors began to close on the empty marble. Daniel put out his hand and held them, and stepped through, into the thinning edge of his own jurisdiction, alone in the cleared center of the world, and went to find a stairwell where he could safely call his wife and tell her a small kind thing about when he'd be home.
