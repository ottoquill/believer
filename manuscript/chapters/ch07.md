## Tamsin

She found him the way you find a leak, by following the stains.

He didn't know that yet. What he knew, that first afternoon, was that a woman he'd never met was sitting across the small table at the back of the coffee place he'd chosen precisely because no one he'd ever worked with would be caught dead in it, and that she had ordered nothing, which in his old life would have told him everything. People who ordered nothing had come to take something. They had decided the meeting before it started.

"Daniel Mercer," she said. Not a question. She'd left a notebook face-down on the table, an actual paper notebook, and a phone beside it screen-up, and he noted the phone the way he noticed all phones now. The recorder was almost certainly running. Let it run. The phone caught only the air between them, and the air between them carried nothing the law could use.

"Ms. Okafor," he said. He'd looked her up in the four hours between her message and this. The Lede. Thirty-seven. A byline that turned up under stories that had cost people their jobs and twice their freedom, in a prose so clean it read like the verdict had already come in. The kind of journalist his old firm built whole strategies around containing — not the credulous ones, who could be fed, but the patient ones, who waited. "You said in your message you were writing about a pattern."

"I am."

"And you think I'm in it."

"I think you're the center of it." She turned the cup that wasn't there, an old habit of someone who interviewed for a living and had trained herself out of fidgeting and into stillness, which was its own kind of fidget. "Six weeks ago a deposition fell apart in a way depositions don't. Three executives, lawyered to the teeth, prepped for days — I've seen the kind of binders they got — and inside forty minutes one of them is volunteering the existence of a second set of invoices nobody asked about. His own lawyer is trying to call a break and the man won't stop. The transcript reads like a confession nobody scheduled." She tipped her head. "You were in the room. Not on the witness list. In the gallery. A consultant, the sign-in sheet says, for a firm that has no record of consulting on it."

"Buildings are full of people."

"They are. So I went looking for other rooms full of people." She said it lightly, but she watched him while she said it, the way he used to watch the back of the press room, for the gust. "A councilman who'd denied a thing for two years recants it standing up, into a hot mic, and afterward tells a reporter he doesn't understand what came over him. A dealership where, for one strange Tuesday, three salesmen in a row tell customers the actual price. A custody hearing where a father stops mid-sentence, says he can't, just can't, and gives the mother everything." She let each one land. "Different rooms. Different people. One thing in common, if you map it. Took me a while to see it, because the thing in common keeps moving."

Daniel kept his face the way he'd kept it for twenty years — open, interested, faintly amused, the face of a man who found the world's theories charming. It cost him nothing to wear it because he wasn't saying anything. He had learned that in the first week, the way you learn the floor plan of a house you've gone blind in: silence cost nothing, and only what you said could hurt you. He could sit here all afternoon producing the face and pay no toll at all.

What he hadn't accounted for was that the face had stopped working.

"You don't have to say anything," she said, gently, almost kindly, and he understood that she had watched him decide to say nothing and had read the decision off him like a chyron. "That's the thing about you, isn't it. I've talked to four people who were in your rooms. Not one of them could tell me a clean lie about that day. They'd start to, and then they'd — " she made a small gesture, a flinch she performed rather than felt — "and they'd reroute. Into the truth, or into not answering. I sat across from a man whose whole career was telling me a comfortable version of events, and he physically could not do it, and he was so frightened of himself that he wept. In a Pret. At eleven in the morning." She paused. "I've never had an interview go like that in my life. And then I had four of them. And all four men had been in a room with the same consultant from the firm that doesn't consult."

A waiter drifted toward them, sensed something, drifted off. Daniel had got used to that — the way people approached him and then declined to, the way a room rearranged itself around him without anyone choosing to, bodies migrating toward the windows, toward the door, toward the soft far edge where the air went ordinary again and a man could tell his wife he'd be home soon and mean to and not be. He was the empty chair at the center of every gathering now. People drifted to the perimeter the way water finds the low place, not knowing why, only knowing that near him the conversation got strangely careful and that careful was tiring.

"Suppose," Daniel said — and he chose the word with care, because a hypothetical was a country he could still travel freely; *suppose* and *what if* paid no toll — "suppose there were such a pattern. Suppose it were even true. Why bring it to the man at the center, and not just print it."

"Because if I print it without him, it's a ghost story." She said this plainly, and he respected the plainness; she wasn't selling. "Four men who got weird in a room. A reporter who thinks she's found a unicorn. My editor reads that and asks if I've been sleeping. I print *that* and the only thing it does is warn whoever you are to go to ground, and I never find you again, and the most important story of my life dies as a thing people roll their eyes at." She turned the absent cup again. "And because — and I'll be honest with you, since apparently that's the house rule now — it's the biggest story there is, and I'm not going to let someone else have it because I was squeamish about coming to the source."

It was, Daniel thought, an admirably constructed approach. She'd given him the flattery and the threat in the same breath and labeled both as candor; she'd told him she could ruin his anonymity and then told him she'd rather not, and dressed the whole transaction in the language of a confession so that refusing her would feel like refusing honesty itself. He recognized the work. It was, in fact, his work, run the other direction — she was reframing him, and doing it well, and he felt toward her the warmth one card counter feels toward another across a table.

"You're managing me," he said.

For the first time something moved in her face that she hadn't put there. "Yes," she said. "Is it working?"

"A little." He heard himself say it and felt nothing, which told him it was true, and the fact that he now learned the truth of his own sentences by whether they hurt was a thing he had not finished hating. "Do something for me first."

"What."

"Lie to me."

She went still in the particular way of a person who suspects a trap and can't yet see its shape. "About what?"

"Anything. Something I can't check. Tell me where you grew up. Tell me you walked here. Just make it false, and make it a thing you know is false while you say it." He kept his voice easy, the voice people trusted on instinct, the instrument and the sin. "I'm not asking to humiliate you. I'm asking because in about four seconds you're going to understand the pattern from the inside, and after that we won't have to do this dance, because you'll know I'm not a unicorn. I'm a fact you walked into."

She held his eyes. She was brave; he'd give her that for the rest of his life. She didn't ask why or stall or laugh it off. She set her jaw the way a person does before cold water, and said, "I grew up in — "

and stopped.

He watched it happen to her. He'd watched it happen to a dozen people now and it never stopped being terrible, the small private catastrophe of it, the way a confident sentence walked off a cliff it couldn't see. Her mouth had already shaped the lie. The lie was loaded. And somewhere behind her eyes a thing she had not consented to slammed a door, and her breath caught, and a fine sweat came up at her hairline as if the room had jumped ten degrees, and her hand went to her temple, just touched it, the way you touch a tooth that's begun to go. Not the nosebleed. A small lie, a where-did-you-grow-up lie, no consequence riding it — a sting only, the foil-taste he could read on her face though he'd never tasted hers, a flashbulb behind the eyes, the body filing an objection it would not let her overrule.

"— I can't," she finished, and the held breath went out of her all at once. "I can't *say* it. I had the word. The word was right there." She pressed two fingers to the corner of her eye, hard, then looked at them as if expecting them to be wet, and they weren't, it hadn't gone that far, the lie had been too small to cost that much. "It's not that I won't. I physically — there's a — " She stopped trying to describe it. People always stopped trying to describe it. The describing was its own small lie, the gap between the word and the thing, and the condition, he had come to suspect, had no patience even for approximations. "What is this."

"I don't know," Daniel said, and this was the truth and it landed in him with the soft thud of all his true sentences, painless, deep. "I genuinely don't. I've stopped trying to know. There's no one to ask. I looked for others. There don't seem to be others." He turned his own absent cup, mirroring her without meaning to. "Everyone in the room is in it. Me, the waiter, you. It reaches as far as my voice and not an inch further, and it doesn't go through phones." He watched her file each fact at the speed of someone who'd waited her whole career to file them. "That last part matters to you, professionally. You can call your editor from the hallway and tell him whatever soothing thing you like. In here, you can't."

"Through a wall?"

"Through a wall, you're free. Distance, you're free. Across a parking lot, lie to your heart's content." He almost smiled. "I think of it as the room. Wherever I am there's a room, and inside it nobody can say the thing they know is false without the body sending the bill."

She had picked up the pen now. She hadn't decided to; her hand had decided for her, the way his hand reached for phones. She wasn't writing. She was holding it the way a smoker holds the unlit cigarette. "And the toll scales," she said slowly, working it as she spoke. "The councilman didn't weep over the price of a sedan. The salesmen — three men, three small fibs, three small — and they came out of it shaken but fine. But the man with the second set of invoices. The deposition man. He wasn't fine."

"No."

"How bad does it get."

And here Daniel paused, because here was the thing he had not said to anyone, the thing he had worked out alone on a harbor rail and not yet been able to set down: that the field did not catch liars. It caught the people honest enough to keep a private account of what they actually knew, and it let the certain ones — the serene, the convinced, the men who had stopped feeling the seam between the world and the world they preferred — walk through untouched. He had the proof of it filed in him like a foreign object. He could feel it now, the edge of it, every time he opened his mouth.

He did not tell her that part. Not yet. That part was the dark engine, and he hadn't decided whether to hand her the key.

"Bad enough that I won't be the one to test the top of it," he said. "There are people who can lie to me and feel nothing. You should know that before you build anything on me. I'm not a truth machine. I'm — " he searched, and found it, and let it out because it was accurate and accuracy was free — "I'm a machine that hurts the honest. The liars who believe themselves walk through me like an open door."

It was the closest thing to an aphorism he'd allowed himself in weeks, and he hated that he'd given it to her, and he watched her not write it down, which was when he understood she was better than he'd assumed, because the ones who wrote it down right away were the ones who'd already decided what the story was.

"Tamsin," she said. "If we're going to do this you might as well use it."

"Are we going to do this."

She set the pen down. Squared the notebook to the table edge, a small ordering. "Here's what I think you want," she said, and now it was her turn to arrange the available facts. "I think you want it told right, once, by someone who won't sell you to a network as a sideshow, because the alternative isn't *quiet* — the alternative is this gets out anyway, badly, and you spend the rest of your life as a magic trick. I think you've already worked out you can't keep it secret, because secrets need lies to keep them and you've run clean out of lies. And I think you're sitting here, at the back of a coffee shop nobody you know would enter, talking to a reporter, because some part of you has already decided to come out and is shopping for the least bad way to do it." She let that sit. "How am I doing."

He felt each of her sentences pass through him looking for a lie to catch on, and find none, because she was right, all of it was right, she had read him the way he used to read a room, and the not-finding-a-lie was its own verdict: he didn't correct her, and in the field his silence was as legible as a signature.

"You're managing me," he said again, "and it's working," and this time neither of them smiled, because it had stopped being a card game somewhere in the last ten minutes and become the other thing, the thing he had not had in a long time and had not known he was starving for — a person across a table who knew exactly what he was and was still sitting there. Nadia knew what he was and couldn't bear to sit there. The world that drifted to the edge of every room he entered knew it in their nerves before their minds and edged away. This woman had walked in, ordered nothing, and stayed in the center on purpose, the only one in weeks who had chosen the center, and the fact that she'd done it to use him did not, he found, subtract from it at all. It might even have been the thing that made it bearable. Use was honest. Use he understood.

"I have terms," he said.

"Of course you do."

"My daughter's name is not in it. Not her school, not her age, not a photograph, nothing a person could thread back to her. She's nine. She didn't ask for any of this and she's the only one of us who can't get out of the room." He heard his voice go somewhere he hadn't sent it and pulled it back. "That one isn't a negotiation. That one I'll walk for."

Tamsin looked at him for a moment with something that wasn't the journalist. "Done," she said. "And it's not me being kind. A nine-year-old in a piece like this turns the reader against me, not toward you. So you get the right answer for the wrong reason." She picked the pen back up. "I'll be honest, since I have to be, in here. Most of what I do for you, I'll be doing because it serves the story. You should know that going in. I'm not your friend. I'm the person who's going to make you famous, and that's a different and worse thing."

"I know exactly what that is," Daniel said. "I did it to people for a living. The other direction." He almost said her name and didn't, saving it. "We'll get along fine. We're the same crime in different keys."

He hadn't meant to say that out loud; it had drifted up from somewhere, a thing he'd thought once about Nadia, and the saying of it cost him nothing because it was true, and the truth of it sat between them on the table like the cup neither of them had ordered.

Tamsin clicked the pen. Outside, the afternoon was going long and gold against the glass, and at the far edge of the little room two students had drifted toward the window without knowing why, and the barista was being unusually, mysteriously frank with a customer about the pastries, and Daniel felt the familiar small loneliness of the field, the way it made an honest desert of every place he stood — and then, across the table, the unfamiliar thing. The company.

"Before we start," she said. "One question, off the record, for me. Not the piece."

"You can't promise off the record in here. You'll feel it if you break it." He nodded at the space between them. "That's the joke of it. You came to interview the one subject you can't lie *to,* and forgot you also can't lie *near.* Ask your question."

She looked at him a long moment, and he saw her weigh whether she wanted an answer she could not later pretend she hadn't gotten.

"Are you a good man," Tamsin Okafor said.

And Daniel Mercer, who could no longer lie, opened his mouth to answer and found that he did not know, that the knowing was gone the way her grew-up-in had been gone, snatched at the threshold — not because the condition forbade the answer but because, for the first time since it began, there was a true sentence he genuinely could not locate inside himself, and the silence that followed was the most honest thing he'd said all day, and they both heard it, and she, mercifully, did not write that down either.
