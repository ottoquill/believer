## The Believer

The first of them came at 6:40, and Daniel knew it was the first only because he was awake to count.

He stood at the kitchen window in the half-dark with coffee he wasn't drinking and watched a man cross the street toward the building the way people cross toward a thing they've rehearsed crossing toward — too directly, hands too still, a man who had decided in the night to come and was now spending the decision. He carried a manila folder against his chest with both arms, the way you carry a child or a wound. He stopped under the awning four floors down and looked up — not at Daniel's window, he couldn't have known the window, but at all of it, as if the building itself were the man he'd come to see — and then he sat on the low granite ledge by the door and set the folder on his knees and waited. The folder was the point. The man had brought his case. He believed — and this was the part that turned the coffee in Daniel's stomach to something colder — that if he could only get into a room with Daniel Mercer, whatever was in the folder would simply rise off the page and stand in the air where everyone could see it. That was the myth. The myth had a folder now, and a wool coat, and it was sitting on his stoop.

By eight there were four.

By the time June had to leave for school there were nine, and a van.

*

The van had Channel 9's logo, though Daniel had spent enough of his life on the other side of that logo to know the logo was the cheap part and would go to anyone with a laminator and a grievance. A woman with very good hair stood in front of it doing the thing where you talk to a camera about a building as if the building were on trial, and Daniel watched her from above with the specific nausea of a man watching an instrument he had played for twenty years now played, badly, on him.

He had done this. Not the standing-outside part — the part before it, the part that made the standing-outside possible. He had spent two decades teaching the world that a person could be rendered down to a phrase that would cling, and now Tamsin had rendered *him* into two words he could not get off no matter how he turned. *The Believer.* He had read the piece four times. It was the best thing ever written about him, which was not a high bar, because nothing had ever been written about him; he had been the hand, never the face. He had built faces. Now he had one, and it wasn't his.

The kicker was the line she'd warned him she was proud of. He could not get it out of his head, which she had also, he suspected, intended. It was a good line. He hated that he could tell it was a good line. He hated more that it wasn't even wrong.

Nadia came in dressed for the hospital, June behind her in the doorway with her backpack already on, and the three of them stood in the kitchen that had once been a still-life of an ordinary family and was now a kind of bunker with marble counters, and nobody said the obvious thing, which was: there are people downstairs, and they are downstairs because of you.

"I'll take her the back way," Nadia said. "Through the garage."

"There isn't a back way out of the garage. It comes out on the same—"

"I know there isn't a back way." She said it flat, not unkind, a woman declining to be corrected about the geography of her own captivity. He had learned, in the weeks since the field, that this was the new shape of their talking: she would say a thing that wasn't quite true the way people say things, as scaffolding, as a place to stand while the next sentence got built. *There isn't a back way.* True enough. *I'll take her the back way.* Not true, and they both knew it, and the Daniel of nine weeks ago would have understood that what she meant was *I will handle this, you have done enough,* and would have nodded. He nodded now. He had at least learned to nod.

"June," he said. "Hey."

She looked at him. She had been looking at him a lot lately, the long appraising look of a child recalibrating an instrument she'd thought she understood. Nine years old, his eyes and Nadia's refusal to be lied to, a combination he thought would make her either a saint or a prosecutor, and he no longer had any say in which.

"Is it because of you," she said. "The people."

He felt the question arrive the way he now felt all questions arrive — as a door opening onto a drop. He could not soften it. He had tried, the first week, the small reflexive mercies, *don't worry about it, sweetheart,* and the foil-taste had bloomed under his tongue, the flashbulb behind the eyes, his body refusing to let him be kind in the only currency he'd ever had. So he had stopped trying to be kind and started trying to be true, which was not the same thing and was sometimes nearly its opposite.

"Yes," he said. "It's because of me."

She nodded, taking it, filing it. "Okay," she said, and shouldered her bag higher and went past him to the garage door, and it was the *okay* that undid him, the flat administrative *okay* of a kid learning to budget around the absence of the comfortable version. Nadia followed. At the door she paused, not turning.

"You should call someone. About the building. They can't just—" and she stopped, because *they can't just* was the kind of sentence that used to be true and wasn't anymore. The world had changed its mind about what they could just do, and she was too honest now — they were all too honest now, it came off him like a draft — to finish a sentence she didn't believe. The door shut. He heard the garage grind up and then, faintly, the rise in pitch of the people outside as the car nosed into them, the way water changes its note around a stone.

*

He called Tamsin from the hallway by the elevators, out of the apartment, out of the field — an instinct so trained into him that he no longer noticed it until he was doing it, leaving the room to make the call the way a man steps outside to smoke. The hallway was the only place left where he could lie, and he had nothing to lie about, and he went there anyway, for the privacy of being a man whose every word didn't cost.

"You saw," Tamsin said.

"I see it from my window. There's a van."

"There's going to be more than a van." She had the brightness of someone whose not-sleeping is the good kind. "Daniel, it's everywhere. It's the only thing. The *Times* is calling, three networks, a man from Justice who wouldn't give a name—" She caught herself. "I'm not going to pretend I'm sorry it landed. I told you I wouldn't."

"I'm not asking you to."

"But I'm sorry about the van." A pause he believed. "I am genuinely sorry about the van."

"There's a man with a folder. He's been on the stoop since quarter to seven. He thinks if he gets in a room with me his folder comes true."

"Does it?"

And there it was — the thing in her, under the friendship, that he'd known was there from the first hour and had decided to keep being her friend anyway, because he was no longer in a position to be choosy about who used him. *Does it.* Not *is he okay.* The reporter's question, the only question, surfacing through the apology like a fin.

"It does and it doesn't," he said. "If he's lying to himself about what's in the folder, it'll hurt him to say it and he won't, and we'll all stand around watching him not say it, which is its own kind of telling. If he believes it, true or not, he'll say it and it'll cost him nothing and prove nothing." He watched the elevator numbers climb and stop. "I'm not a machine that reads paper, Tamsin. I'm a room people bleed in. That's the whole of it."

"That," she said slowly, the way she said things she was already typing in her head, "is exactly what people are afraid you'll figure out how to charge for."

*

He let the man with the folder up.

He shouldn't have. He knew, descending in the elevator, that the correct move was the lawyer, the statement, the managed nothing — *Mr. Mercer is unable to comment on individual matters,* a sentence engineered to be both a door and a wall. But the man had been there six hours, the cold had gone into his folder, the manila soft at the corners, and Daniel found he could not perform the managed nothing on a person he could see. The field had done that too. It had always been easier to be cruel through a screen; the whole architecture of his old life had run on the mercy of distance, and the field had taken distance away.

The man's name was Howell. Perhaps sixty, a retired insurance adjuster, and the folder was about his brother. They sat in the lobby because Daniel would not take a stranger upstairs, in two chairs by the dead fountain the building kept for show, and the doorman drifted toward the far end of his desk, toward the edge of the room, the way they all did now without knowing they did it.

"Forty-one years," Howell said. "My brother and me, we haven't spoken in forty-one years. Everybody thinks it's money. It wasn't money." The folder lay open on his knees and he wasn't looking at it. "He told a lie about me. To our father, before our father died. Told him I'd said terrible things, that I'd wished him dead so I'd get the house. I never said it. I never thought it. And the old man died believing it — died thinking his older boy wanted him in the ground." His hands shook on the soft folder. "I read about you. I thought, if I bring him here. If I get him in a room with you. He can't keep saying it was true, can he. Whatever you are."

"What do you want him to do," Daniel said.

"I want him to take it back." Howell looked up, his eyes wet and furious and seventy years old in a sixty-year-old face. "I want him to say *I lied,* and feel it, and I want to be there when he feels it."

And Daniel, who had spent his life arranging what people should never say to each other, looked at this man who had walked through the cold to bring him a forty-one-year wound and a hope, and felt the cold thing he used to call *interest* turn into something with no name he liked. Because he could see the scene the way he used to see press conferences before they happened. The brother arriving, two old men in a room, the field working, the field always worked — and Howell did not understand, could not be made to understand in the time they had, that there were two ways this went and only one was the way he'd come for.

"Mr. Howell. I have to tell you something you're not going to like, and I'm telling you because in a room with me I can't tell you anything else." He set his hands flat on his knees. "A man who carries a lie like that for forty-one years — there's a good chance he doesn't experience it as a lie anymore. You tell a thing often enough, need it true badly enough, and it becomes true to you. And a man who's made it true in himself can say it in front of me and feel nothing. He'll say it clean. And you'll be standing there having brought your brother across the city to watch him not flinch."

Howell stared at him.

"I can only make a man bleed for the lies he still knows are lies. The ones he's turned into truth in his own head go right past me. Those are the ones that did the most damage, and I'm worse than useless against them. I'll make him *look* honest."

It was, Daniel realized as he said it, the truest description of his condition he had managed to give anyone, including himself, and it cost him nothing at all, because every word was true, and the not-costing was itself a kind of grief.

Howell closed the folder slowly, with both hands, the way he'd opened it. "So you can't help me."

"Go to him without me. Sit in his kitchen. He might tell you the truth there, in his own room, where it costs him nothing but courage. That's the only place it's ever worked. It was working long before I came along."

*

But it wasn't that clean, and Daniel knew it wasn't, because three days later there was a different man, and that one Daniel did not turn away, and that one came back.

He came back with his daughter.

Daniel had it secondhand — from the doorman, then the building manager, then, worst, from the news, which had a name now and a clip: fourteen seconds filmed on a phone that would outlive everyone in it. A woman in her thirties had brought her father to stand near the building. Not to get in. Just to stand in the radius, because the radius was the thing now; people had worked out that you didn't need the man, you needed the room, and the room had edges you could crowd against on a public sidewalk if you were brave or desperate enough. She had brought her father to the edge of the field to ask him, in front of strangers and a phone, whether he had done the thing he had spent her childhood swearing he had not done.

And he had. And he had not lied to himself about it — that was the cruelty, the thing the loophole would have spared a worse man. He had known all along, every day, exactly what he'd done, and so the field gave him no shelter, and standing on a public sidewalk with his daughter's phone in his face he had pressed the back of his hand to his nose and it had come away dark, and he had said *yes,* just *yes,* and walked very fast away with the blood on his cuff, and the daughter had not gone after him. She had stood with the phone still up, filming the empty sidewalk where her father had been, getting, at last, after thirty years, her answer. Daniel watched the fourteen seconds on his own screen, in his own apartment, and understood that he had done that. Not the man's crime. The *witnessing.* He had made the city a place where you could walk your father to the edge of the truth and watch him bleed it out and have it on your phone before he reached the corner.

The good was real. He told himself the good was real, and it was — somewhere across town a man named Marcus Reyes was breathing free air for the first time in eleven years because a witness could not hold a lie in a room Daniel sat in, and that had never been false. But the good and the harm came out of the same opened seam. You could not have the one without splitting the world the same way for the other, and the people who flooded toward him did not sort themselves into the deserving and the cruel before they arrived. They arrived as people. With folders. With daughters. With wounds forty-one years old that he could not heal and three days old that he could not stop bleeding.

*

That evening June came home and would not eat.

"What happened," Nadia said, and June said *nothing,* and the word sat in the kitchen, in the field, intact, unpunished, and Daniel watched his wife and his daughter both register that the *nothing* hadn't hurt her, that she'd said it clean, and he watched Nadia's face do something he would think about for a long time afterward — relief and fear braided so tight he couldn't have pulled them apart. Relief that the child was unhurt. Fear of what it meant that she was.

Because *nothing* was a lie. He had it later, in pieces, the way he got everything now and never softened: a boy named Tyler had asked her at lunch whether her dad could tell when *she* was lying, and the table had laughed, and a girl had said her mother said the Mercers were *dangerous,* that you shouldn't be in a room with people like that, and another had said her father said the Believer was *blessed,* a *sign,* and June had sat in the middle of it being neither dangerous nor blessed, being nine, being a kid whose lunch had become a referendum, and when the teacher asked what was wrong she had said *nothing,* and it had not hurt her, and now she was home saying it again and it still didn't hurt.

And Daniel saw, with a clarity that arrived like the foil-taste, like a flashbulb, that his daughter had begun to learn the only thing the new world had to teach: that *nothing* was a sayable word, an unpunished word, if you could get yourself to half-believe it. That you could be safe. You just had to stop knowing what had happened to you.

She ate three bites and asked to be excused, and Nadia let her go, and the two of them sat at the island where the marriage had cracked nine weeks before, under the drawing still magneted to the refrigerator — three figures, a yellow sun, WE ARE A HAPPY FAMILY in the careful staggered capitals of nine, with its erased and unerasable question mark pressing through the paper where the eraser hadn't quite won. Daniel looked at the word *nothing* still hanging in the air where his daughter had set it down without bleeding.

*That's the lesson,* he thought. Not mine. I came to make people unable to lie. The first thing the world learned from me was how to do it without it hurting.

Outside, four floors down, the man with the wool coat had gone, or another had taken his place; from this height, in the dark, the figures at the edge of the lamplight were indistinguishable, a small patient crowd gathered at the boundary of the thing they had been told would set them free, leaning a little outward, every one of them, the way they all leaned now, toward the edge of the room.
