## Demand

The subpoena arrived by a man in a good suit who could not, on Daniel's stoop, manage to say good morning without his jaw working strangely, as if he'd bitten down on something sharper than the word deserved.

"Compelled appearance," the man got out, and held the paper at arm's length the way you'd hold a thing you didn't want to be seen owning. Then, because he was close enough now and the question was already in his face: "Is it true you can— " and he stopped, and swallowed, and Daniel watched him decide not to finish a sentence that would have required him to commit to a belief he didn't have. The man left fast. They always left fast now. Daniel had learned to read the speed of a departure the way a doctor reads a pulse.

He took the paper inside. *The People v. someone he'd never heard of,* a defendant in a fraud trial across the river, and the request was not for his testimony — he had nothing to testify to — but for his *presence,* his body, in the gallery, during the cross-examination of a witness whose account both sides apparently agreed was load-bearing and neither side trusted. The defense wanted him there to break the witness. The prosecution, Daniel would learn within the hour, had filed its own motion to keep him out, on the grounds that his presence in the room constituted — the phrase was lovely, he'd have admired it in another life — *an unconstitutional pressure on the free formation of testimony.* As if testimony had ever been free. As if the whole apparatus of oath and bailiff and the flag behind the judge weren't already a machine built to make lying expensive, and his only crime was making it expensive in a currency people could feel.

By noon there were three more. A wrongful-death plaintiff who wanted him in a deposition. A divorce — a *divorce,* the lawyer's letter practically panting — that wanted him present while the husband answered questions about a second account in a second city. And a motion, filed by a man Daniel had never met, to *bar* Daniel from a courthouse in a county he had never visited, preemptively, in case the other side got the idea first. He was being fought over in rooms he wasn't in, by people arguing about what his body did to other bodies, and not one of the filings used his name without the word *asset* or *instrument* somewhere near it, as if the law had already decided he was a thing before it decided whose.

By the end of the week he had a lawyer — a careful, sleepless woman named Marsh whom Gideon had sent over with the air of a man handing a friend a single sandbag and wishing him luck — and she had told him, gently, that the filings were not the problem. The filings were only the symptom. The problem was the thing the law had just discovered it could want.

---

Renata Holm did not file anything. That was the first thing Daniel noticed about her, and he noticed it the way he'd once noticed which reporters didn't take the free coffee: it told you they'd budgeted for the long version.

She came in person, alone, on a Tuesday, in a coat that cost what his old coats had cost and concealed it better. She declined to sit until he sat. She was perhaps fifty, with the unhurried competence of someone who had spent a career in rooms where the stakes were real and the lights were bad, and she put a single card on his kitchen island and nothing else — no folder, no aide, no recorder visible, though Daniel assumed the recorder, because you always assumed the recorder, it was only polite.

"I'm not going to lie to you," she said, and then, before he could do anything with the line, she smiled at it, a small dry private smile. "I understand that's a strange thing to promise a man like you. I mean it differently than people usually mean it. In your house I *can't.* So I've thought hard about what I'm willing to say."

"Most people just don't talk," Daniel said.

"Most people aren't trying to recruit you." She let that sit. "I work for the Department. I'm not going to characterize which part, partly because I can't and partly because the characterization would bore us both. I'll tell you what I think is true, and you'll know it's true, because you'd know if it weren't. That's the entire reason I came in person instead of sending a letter. A letter from me would be worth nothing. You've discovered that already, I think. That you've become the only place in the country where a document can't help me."

Daniel said nothing. Silence was free; refusing to speak cost him not a thing. And his refusals had become, to other people, almost unbearable, because a silence in his kitchen was as legible as a polygraph needle and everyone knew it.

"There are men in custody," Holm said, "whom we cannot question. Not because they won't talk — they'll talk all day. Because what they say can't be trusted and we have no way, none, to separate the part that's real from the part that's theater. We have spent — I'm going to be vague here, not to deceive you, I literally don't have the figure — an enormous amount of money on ways to know whether a frightened man is telling us the truth, and all of it is garbage. Polygraphs are garbage. Drugs are garbage and illegal besides. Rapport, patience, the whole craft of it — it works one time in five and we can never tell which one. And then you." She turned the card a quarter-turn on the granite, squaring it. "You walk into a room and the problem we have spent seventy years failing to solve simply stops existing. People near you cannot lie. Do you understand what that is worth? Not to me. To people who will live or not live depending on whether we knew a thing in time."

"You're telling me it's for the saving of lives," Daniel said.

"I'm telling you I believe that." A beat; she felt the distinction land and let him see her feel it. "Which is not the same sentence, and you know the difference better than anyone alive. I'm not in pain saying it because I do believe it. I'd be careful, if I were you, about how much comfort to take in who flinches around you and who doesn't. The worst people I've ever met were the most comfortable in rooms like this one."

It was the most honest thing anyone had said to him in weeks, and it was a threat, and the two were not in tension. He felt the old sharpening stir — the thing he used to get from a room that could still go either way — and he disliked feeling it, because it meant some part of him had already begun the work, arranging her, reading the grain.

"I'm not for sale," he said, and felt nothing, because it was true, and was unsettled by how little that proved.

"I haven't named a price." Holm stood. She left the card. "I'm not going to push. I came to be a face you'd remember instead of a letter you'd throw out. When the courts are done fighting over you — and they will be done, badly, in a way that helps no one — you may find that a quiet arrangement with people who actually understand what you are looks better than being a circus exhibit subpoenaed by every divorce lawyer on the eastern seaboard. That's all. I think you'll come to it on your own. The people who come to it on their own do better." At the door she paused. "I won't say it was nice to meet you. You'd hear it." And she let herself out, and Daniel sat for a while with the card he did not pick up, and understood that she had not lied once, and that this was the most frightening sales call he had ever received, precisely because the salesman had been forced, in his presence, to mean every word.

---

Victor Salt arrived the way weather arrives.

He came with people — that was the difference, that was everything. Holm had come alone because she understood the room. Salt came with a personal physician and a woman whose title Daniel never caught and two young men in identical soft sweaters who arranged themselves, the instant they crossed the threshold, against the far walls — iron filings finding the rim of a field, suddenly and unanimously fascinated by the view out the windows. Daniel had stopped finding it funny months ago. What it was now was lonely: a room that rearranged itself to leave him at the empty center, a man everyone wanted near and no one could bear to stand beside.

Salt stood near, though. Daniel gave him that. Salt walked to the middle of the kitchen and put both hands flat on the island and grinned at him like a man who'd found a coin worth more than the field he'd bought it with.

"Do you know," Salt said, "you are the most interesting thing to happen to epistemology since the printing press? I mean that. I had a man write me a memo. *Epistemology.* I read the whole thing." He was younger than Daniel expected and dressed worse, deliberately, the costume of a man who no longer had to signal anything, which was its own loud signal. "Here's the situation, Daniel. May I call you Daniel. Here's the situation. You're a wonder and you're a problem and the wonder and the problem are the same fact, which is that the *effect*"—he liked the word, he turned it over—"the effect lives in *you,* in one mortal nervous system, in a man who is, no offense, going to get hit by a bus someday, statistically, like everyone, and when that happens the single most valuable phenomenon on the planet just—" he snapped, softly "—stops. That's intolerable. From a stewardship standpoint that's almost criminal. So."

"So," Daniel said.

"So we study it. Properly. Funded, instrumented, with the best people, and not in some federal basement where a woman in a nice coat decides which terrorists get to meet you." He'd known about Holm, then; of course he had. "We figure out what it *is.* And then — this is the part the memo got excited about — then maybe we figure out how to *make* it. Not the curse. The good part. The part where people stop lying. Imagine it as a— call it a discipline. A practice. A thing you can install. We're calling it—" he glanced at the woman whose title Daniel hadn't caught; she said nothing; he said it anyway "—synthetic conviction. You believe what you say. You say what you believe. You feel no pain because there's no gap. We close the gap. For everyone. At scale."

Daniel had spent twenty years listening to men describe the future in order to sell the present, and he recognized the music, the way Salt landed on *scale* the way the governor had landed on *legitimate,* a word doing the labor of a proof. But there was something underneath it this time that Daniel hadn't heard from Holm. Holm wanted to *use* the gap between what men said and what they knew. Salt wanted to *abolish* it — thought the field a flaw in the human design that he, finally, had the budget to fix.

"You want to teach people to believe their own lies," Daniel said. "So it won't hurt."

"I want to *end the lying,*" Salt said, and he said it with his hand on his heart, and he felt no pain at all, and Daniel watched him feel no pain and could not have told a court whether the man was sincere or simply did not care enough about the truth of it to register the gap — the bullshitter's grace, the partial shielding of the man for whom the question of true-or-false had simply never been load-bearing. Either way the body told Daniel nothing, and Daniel understood, standing in his own kitchen, that here was a kind of person his condition could not touch at all, and that there were going to be more of them, and that some of them were going to be very rich.

"I'll send a number," Salt said, when he saw the meeting was over, which he saw quickly; the stupid did not get this far. "A number that will embarrass you. Not because I think you're for sale. Because I think you're tired, and broke, which I checked, and because there's a little girl upstairs"—Daniel's face must have done something, because Salt lifted both hands, *easy, easy*—"who I am noting *exists,* that's all, the way I note that buses exist. I'm not threatening your kid, Daniel, Christ, what kind of— I'm saying you have responsibilities and I have resources and those two facts are going to find each other eventually. They always do." He nodded toward his drifting people, who peeled off the walls in relief. "Think about epistemology. It's wild down there." And the weather left, and took its physician and its sweaters and its barometric pressure with it, and the room felt, after, both larger and worse.

---

Tamsin found him on the roof, where he'd taken to going because the field meant nothing up there with no one in it and because the harbor at this distance was just lights and could not lie or be lied to.

"You let Salt in," she said. She'd come up the stairs angry; he could hear it in the consonants. She stayed near the door, at the edge, then caught herself doing it and made herself cross the gravel to stand beside him at the parapet, which he noted and did not mention, because the people who fought the instinct were the ones worth keeping.

"I let everyone in," Daniel said. "It's a very busy kitchen."

"Holm too."

"How do you know about Holm." He turned. "You're not supposed to know about Holm. I didn't tell you about Holm."

"That's a *yes,* then, thank you." She said it flat, without triumph, and he saw that she'd guessed and he'd just confirmed it, in his own field, on his own roof, where his face was as good as a signed statement. She had the decency to look unhappy about how she'd gotten it. "Daniel. I want to say this where you can hear that I mean it, so let me get it right." She put her hands on the cold rail. "Two months ago you were a man with a condition. Right now you're an *asset class.* The court fight is going to settle into precedent — they'll work out some rule about when they can drag you in and when they can't, and it'll be incoherent, and it'll stand, and that's almost the least of it. The least of it. Because once the courts have a rule, the rule is a *door,* and Holm walks through doors, and Salt buys the buildings the doors are in. You're not going to get to be a person again. I'm sorry. I'm the one who—" She stopped. Up here she could have lied; up here it would have cost her nothing. She didn't. "I'm the one who named you. I put the door in. I keep thinking about that."

"You wrote what was true."

"I wrote what was *true* and *useful* and I'd be lying"—she almost smiled at the word, here, where she could have used it freely—"if I said I didn't notice it was both. That's the part of the job nobody warns you about. The story that's true and the story that helps you are usually different stories, and the days they're the same story are the days you do your best work and feel the worst." She looked at him sidelong. "Sound like anyone you used to be?"

It did. It landed exactly where she'd placed it, and Daniel felt the narrow professional respect of a man recognizing his own method turned on him by someone good enough to do it gently. He let it sit. The harbor went on being lights.

"Holm didn't lie to me," he said finally. "Not once. I keep— it should have made it easier and it made it worse. Salt didn't feel a thing. He put his hand on his heart and felt *nothing,* Tamsin. And I stood there knowing it meant either he's the most honest man I've met this year or he's so far gone he doesn't track the difference, and the terrible part is it doesn't matter which, because they look the same to me now. From in here." He tapped his own chest, where the field lived, or didn't; where the metal taste came from when he reached for a thing he knew was false. "I used to be able to read a room. It was the whole of what I was. And the one I've got — the only room I'll ever have again — I can't read it at all. I can tell you who's lying. I can't tell you who's good. It turns out those were never the same list."

He had not meant to say so much. The roof did that; the safety of it loosened him, the way a confessional loosens a man precisely because the screen is between him and the eyes. He shut his mouth.

Tamsin didn't write it down. He watched her decide not to, watched the reporter lose, narrowly, to the friend, and he understood that this too was a kind of mercy and that it cost her, the way mercy always cost the truthful something, and that she'd given it anyway.

"They're going to keep coming," she said. "The courts, the Department, Salt, the next ones, the ones with no card and no memo and no manners. You know that."

"I know that."

"What are you going to do?"

He didn't answer, because the honest answer was *I don't know* and even that felt like too much standing here. Below them the city went about the work it could only do at this distance — the deals, the apologies, the small warm necessary untruths, all of it humming safely out past the soft edge of him, the whole free country of liars going about its business in the lit windows, untouchable, because he wasn't in the room. He thought of the subpoena on his counter, *compelled appearance,* the law reaching for his body because his body had become a venue. Holm's card squared on the granite. Salt's number that would embarrass him. The divorce lawyer who wanted to rent him by the hour. None of them wanted Daniel. They had stopped, some weeks ago and all at once, wanting Daniel. What they wanted was the radius around him — the ten honest meters he carried like a weather system he couldn't switch off — and they would go on contesting it in courts and basements and boardrooms until somebody owned it, and the thing he turned over in the cold until it gave up its shape was that the somebody was never going to be him.

"I'm not a witness," he said, mostly to the harbor. "I'm a jurisdiction. They're just arguing about who gets to set the borders."

Tamsin let it stand a moment, because it was the kind of line that earned a moment. Then, gently, the way you correct someone you've decided to keep: "Borders get fought over because somebody lives inside them, Daniel."

He didn't have an answer for that either. They stood at the parapet a while longer, two people in the one place his curse couldn't reach, watching a city tell itself the lies it needed to make it through the night, and somewhere below them a court clerk was already date-stamping the next demand, and another after that, all of them addressed to a man and meant for the country he'd become.
