## The Summer of Tents

The first one had been a single flatbed in an autumn hayfield. By the next summer the tents went up across the interior the way mushrooms go up after rain, everywhere at once and faintly obscene in their suddenness, and Daniel watched them go up the way the rest of the country did, which was on a screen, from a distance, in the one place they could not reach him and he could not reach them.

He watched from the apartment, with the blinds half down against a heat the glass made worse. He watched a great deal now, because watching was the one thing left that asked nothing of his body and cost it nothing. The feed gave him a field in a flat green state he had flown over a hundred times and never once landed in, and on it a tent the size of a regional airport, white and ribbed and swaying faintly at the peak, and inside it perhaps nine thousand people in their good clothes with their hands raised, and the thing Daniel could not stop watching was not the hands. It was the faces.

He had spent twenty years reading faces for the tell. The swallow, the eye that drifts up and to the left, the small involuntary settling that meant a person had privately revised a story. He had built a fortune on the catalogue of those tells, had taught governors to suppress them and lawyers to perform their absence, and these faces, nine thousand of them, had none. They were smooth. A woman near the camera was singing something with her whole chest and her eyes shut and her face had the untroubled slack of a sleeping child's, and Daniel understood, with the slow recognition of a man finding his own handwriting on a document he did not remember signing, that she felt no pain because there was nothing left in her to hurt. She believed every word she sang. She had practiced believing it. She had gone to work on her own mind with the patience of a woman sanding a floor, hour after hour, until she got it smooth.

The chyron under her said *THE CONVICTION — DAY 3 — ATTENDANCE EST. 40,000.* Then the picture cut to Crane on the flatbed, sleeves rolled, no jacket, talking across the heads of forty thousand people the way he had talked across four hundred, as if to one man over a fence, and Daniel turned the sound off because he did not need the sound. He had heard it once at the source and it had been playing in him ever since, low, like a refrigerator he could not locate and switch off.

---

He had tried, in the weeks after the hayfield, to do the thing he was supposed to do.

He had given an interview. Tamsin had set it up and warned him against it in the same breath, which was the most honest she knew how to be about a thing she also wanted, and he had sat in a studio with cold lighting and a host who had practiced his frown, and he had said, carefully, the true things. That a man without pain had proved nothing except his own sincerity. That a person could believe a thing with his whole heart and the thing could still bury eleven people. That the surest way to feel no pain was not to stop lying but to stop knowing, and that Elias Crane was selling the suffering the destruction of their own judgment and calling it grace.

It was the most truth he had spoken in public in his life, and it cost him nothing, because he believed every word, and that was the trouble with it.

The clip ran for eleven seconds on the cable shows and then it was gone, and what stayed, what the feed kept and replayed and cut into the openings of segments, was a still frame of his face mid-sentence, the grey man, the famous unsmiling mouth, captioned by whoever captioned such things: *THE BELIEVER WARNS AGAINST "BELIEF."* And underneath, the comments, which Daniel had promised Nadia he would stop reading and read anyway, in the bathroom, with the fan on. *of course he doesn't want us free. then we don't need him.* *the doubter is scared. pain is doubt leaving the body, even the bible man knows it.* *He believes nothing. We are the believers now.* That last one he recognized; it was Tamsin's, the kicker from her first piece, the line that had named him, lifted now and turned and aimed back at him like a knife taken off a table where you had set it down yourself.

He had said the truest thing he had, into the largest microphone he would ever be handed, and the country had taken it and made it mean its opposite, calmly, the way water takes the shape of any vessel, and there was nothing in him with which to object, because objecting was a thing you did with words, and the words went out of the room he was in and onto the screens where his field could not follow them and where they stopped being his.

That was the part he had not understood until the summer. A man could not lie in Daniel's presence. But a man could walk twelve feet down the hall, out the soft edge of the field, and say anything at all, and the saying carried; and a recording made anywhere and played anywhere carried; and the feed was nothing but recordings and distance, ten thousand miles of soft edge, the whole country standing just outside the room. He had thought, in the early weeks, that he was a jurisdiction. He saw now that he was a very small one. He governed the air for thirty feet. Everything that mattered happened farther away than that.

---

Marcus called him in the third week of it, which surprised him, because Marcus did not call.

"You watching this," Marcus said. Not a question. There was traffic behind him; he was outside somewhere, a payphone of a man, always finding the loud open places, eleven years of ceilings having given him a permanent thirst for sky.

"I'm watching it."

"They had one here. Other side of the river, the old fairgrounds. My cousin went." A pause, the traffic. "She came back different, man. Light. Said it was the most peace she's had since her boy passed. I asked her did she believe the boy was in heaven and she said yes and I said you didn't used to and she said —" He stopped. Daniel heard him decide whether to say it. "She said, *I do now. I practiced.* Like it was a recipe. Like she'd learned to make a pie."

"Marcus."

"I'm not asking you to do anything. I know you can't do anything." This was the thing about Marcus, the thing the others never managed; he never asked Daniel to be more than he was. "I just wanted to say it to somebody whose face wasn't gonna do the thing. You know the thing. Where they hear you're worried and they get worried you're a doubter." He laughed, short. "World's upside down, brother. Used to be the liars hid. Now it's the people still telling the truth, hiding their faces so nobody clocks 'em flinching."

After he hung up Daniel sat a long time with the phone warm in his hand, the sacred object, the last private place, and thought about Marcus's cousin learning to make a pie out of her dead son's salvation, and felt the thing he had felt watching the woman sing — not contempt, he had no contempt left, it had burned off somewhere around the second month — but a kind of vertigo, the floor of the argument dropping away. Because the cousin was not lying. The field would have certified her. She had said *I do now* and meant it, fully, and she was, by every measure Daniel's own body could enforce, telling the truth, and her face was smooth, and her grief was bearable for the first time in a year, and who in the world was Daniel Mercer, of all the men alive, to stand in front of that and call it a lie.

He had spent his career making the false feel true to people because it served his clients. Crane was making the false feel true to people because it eased their suffering. Daniel could not, that afternoon, with the heat coming through the glass, locate the line between the two with any confidence he would have staked money on, and the inability frightened him more than the tents did.

---

What undid him was not the strangers. It was June.

She came in from school in the last week of the term and dropped her bag and said, not looking at him, in the over-casual voice she used for things she had rehearsed, "Dad. Is it true that if you believe something hard enough it stops hurting to say it."

He turned from the window. Nine years old, sunburned across the nose, a scab on one knee. A girl who had spent her whole conscious life as the only person in the country who could get true answers out of the adults in a room, and who had learned to ask for them like a prosecutor, plainly, leaving no exit. She had been bullied at school for what he was. He knew that. He had not been able to spare her any of it, because sparing required the small untruths he could no longer manufacture and she could no longer be fooled by.

"Where did you hear that," he said, which was not an answer, and she knew it wasn't, and her eyes came up.

"Mara's family goes. To the church. The new one." She picked at the scab. "Mara says her mom doesn't get the headaches anymore. The ones from saying nice things. She says you just have to believe it all the way down and then it's not a lie anymore, it's just a thing you believe, and it doesn't hurt." A beat. "Is that true?"

He could have lied to her. The thought arrived with the old reflex behind it, the florist's reflex, the available facts arranging themselves into the kind shape — *no, sweetheart, that's not how it works* — and the arrangement got as far as his throat and stopped, because it was a lie, it was precisely a lie, the cruelest kind, the kind you tell a child for her own protection, and it would have lanced him for it, a sting, a bitten-foil bright little punishment, deserved.

So he told her the truth, which was the only thing the room would let either of them do.

"Yes," he said. "That part's true. If a person really believes a thing, all the way down, then saying it doesn't hurt them. Even if it's not so."

June considered this with the terrible thoroughness of a child running a new rule to its conclusion. He watched her do it. He watched her arrive, in something under ten seconds, at the place it had taken the whole country a summer to reach, and he could not stop her, because stopping her would have required him to say it wasn't so, and it was so.

"So Mara's mom isn't lying," June said slowly. "Even when she says a nice thing that isn't real. Because she believes it. So it doesn't hurt her."

"Yes."

"So if I —" She stopped. She looked at the floor, and then at the window, and her face did a thing he had never seen it do, a smoothing, a deliberate easing, a nine-year-old trying on a posture she had decided to want. "So I could just believe everything's okay. And then it would be okay. And it wouldn't hurt to say."

There it was. He had spent the summer watching forty thousand strangers do it on a screen and now it was happening on his own kitchen floor, in miniature, in his daughter, the loophole arriving in her the way it arrived in everyone, dressed as relief. And he understood that he was watching the single most important thing that had happened in his house since the morning his curse came, and that he was powerless against it in exactly the way he was powerless against Crane, because what was forming in June was not a lie. It was a belief. And his whole monstrous gift, the field that could break a witness and crack a deposition and bless a prophet, could do nothing whatsoever to a belief, could not touch it, could not reach it, would not so much as raise a sweat on the brow of a child who had decided to feel no pain.

"June," he said, and his voice was not steady, which she heard.

"Don't," she said. Quietly. Not crying. Drier than he was. "Don't say the thing. I know you have to say true things. But you don't have to say that one. You're allowed to just not say it." She had learned this young, the legibility of his silences, and she used it now to wall off the one room in herself he might still have gotten into. "I'm just gonna believe it's okay. It's easier. You can't even tell me not to, because you'd have to say it isn't, and then *you'd* get hurt." She picked her bag up off the floor. "So we just won't talk about it."

And she went down the hall to her room, a small straight-backed figure carrying her own salvation like a thing she'd learned to make. He did not call after her. She was right; she was exactly right; the only thing he had to offer was a truth that would cost them both and change nothing. So he let her go, and his silence in the empty kitchen was, as ever, completely legible. What it confessed this time was that his nine-year-old daughter had defeated him with the same instrument a fifty-two-year-old preacher had used in a hayfield, and that the instrument was the one thing in the world he had been built to make and could no longer survive making.

---

That night the feed showed the largest tent yet, somewhere farther in, an arena now, not a field, the white peak swapped for a roof and jumbotrons the size of houses, and on the screens within the screen Crane's face, enormous, the pale untroubled eyes, the kindly squint, and below him a sea of upturned painless faces, and the chyron said *EST. 60,000,* and the sound, when Daniel let it back in for a moment, was the sound of sixty thousand people not flinching, which is to say no sound at all, a great clean roaring nothing, applause and singing and the absence of a single wince in the whole roaring house, because the field could not reach them, because there was no field, because Crane had built the one room in the country where the truth could not get in, and the room held sixty thousand, and there were more rooms going up every night across the whole green interior, and Daniel Mercer, the man they had called the Believer, the fire that Crane had promised would warm the faithful and spare no one else, sat alone in the dark of a high glass apartment thirty feet of jurisdiction wide, and watched the comfortable win.

He thought of Gideon, dead to him now, and the thing Gideon had said over champagne on the last good night of his old life: that there were no facts, only the story nobody had bothered to beat yet. He had admired it then as craft. It had been prophecy. Crane had simply found the larger client — had taken the whole suffering country and reframed its agony as its cleansing, sixty thousand at a sitting, the way Daniel had once managed a hundred and forty. The only difference left was that Daniel had always known and Crane did not, and in the new world that difference protected no one. It only decided which of them paid.

He turned the feed off. The glass went black and gave him back his own room, his own grey reflected face, the lamp, the empty couch where Nadia did not sit. Down the hall June's light was off. She had put herself to bed. She was, he had to assume, already practicing — lying in the dark, sanding her own small mind toward the smoothness that would let her live in his house without bleeding. He could not go in and stop her. Even if he found the words, the words would only hurt her. So the most loving act available to the one man on earth who could not lie was to say nothing, and let his daughter learn to.

He sat in the dark a while longer with the dead screen in front of him. Somewhere past the harbor, past the soft thirty-foot edge of everything he could affect, the tents were full and the faces were smooth and the singing went up untouched into the summer night. And the truth — his truth, the only thing he had ever been given to give — lay where he'd set it down, in a room nobody would come into anymore. Everyone had found a warmer one.
