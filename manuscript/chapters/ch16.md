## The Department

The woman who came for him did not lie once in two hours, which was how Daniel knew she had done her reading.

She arrived on a Tuesday, alone, with a single thin folder and a government car idling four floors down where he could see it from the window. She sat in the chair June used for homework and declined the coffee, the water, the offer of a place to put her coat, and she did all of it without performing any of it, the way Daniel had once taught governors to look interrupted at honest work. She was perhaps fifty, with the unspectacular competence of someone who had spent a career being underestimated and had stopped minding, because the underestimation was useful. She set the folder on her knee and did not open it.

"Renata Holm," she said. "We spoke in the spring. You won't remember the others with me. I made sure you wouldn't."

He remembered. The first approach had been months ago, three people from an office that did not put its name on the door, and Daniel — newly public, frightened, flattered in the way of a man who has just discovered the state would like to keep him — had said no, gently, the way you decline a second drink, and they had thanked him and gone, and he had thought that was the shape of it. A door opened. A door closed. He had not understood that the state did not open doors. The state owned the building.

"I read your interview," she said. "The studio. You were right about everything and it changed nothing, and I think that's the worst kind of right. I want you to know I'm not here because of the preacher. The preacher is a problem for other people, public-affairs people, the kind who think the answer to a man with a microphone is a better man with a microphone." She had a flat midwestern way with her vowels, the interior in her mouth, and it made the things she said sound less like argument than like weather report. "I'm here because of what you can do in a room. Which the preacher, whatever he is, cannot."

"I can't do anything in a room," Daniel said, and felt the small clean nothing of a true sentence. "That's the part everyone gets wrong. I don't do it. It does it. I'm furniture. I'm a smoke detector that won't shut off."

"A smoke detector that won't shut off," Holm said, "is the most valuable object in a burning building." She let that sit. She had the patience of a person who has learned that silence in a room is itself a kind of testimony, and Daniel, who had built a fortune on the same knowledge, recognized a colleague and disliked her for it. "May I tell you what I do? It'll go faster if you know."

---

She told him what she did. She did it plainly, and the plainness was the thing he would remember, the way she described a thing built to break people as if it were a logistics problem, which to her it was.

There were people, she said, whom the country needed to question and could not. Not because they would not talk — everyone talked, eventually — but because the talk would be worthless, a wall of professionally coached untruth, the same untruth Daniel had spent twenty years manufacturing for governors and aerospace concerns, and no instrument on earth could reliably tell it from the true. Polygraphs were theater. Enhanced methods — she used the phrase without flinching, which was its own kind of flinch — produced confessions to anything, which was the same as confessions to nothing. The whole apparatus of getting truth out of an unwilling human being was, she said, a four-thousand-year-old failure everyone in her line had agreed to keep failing at, because the alternative was admitting they were blind.

"And then you," she said. "And then a man walks into a room and inside that room, for thirty feet in every direction, nobody can say a thing they know to be false without it costing them. Do you understand what you are to me? You're the end of four thousand years of guessing."

"People can still refuse to talk," Daniel said. "Silence doesn't hurt. I've spent a year learning what doesn't hurt."

"Silence doesn't hurt," Holm agreed. "But silence in your room is the loudest thing a person can do. You know that better than I do." She watched him not answer. "There it is. You just did it. You went quiet, and I learned something." She almost smiled; it didn't reach anywhere. "That's the instrument. Not that you make them confess — you can't make anyone do anything, you said so, and you're right. You make refusal *legible.* You turn every silence into a sentence. A man who won't answer in your room has answered, and that's worth more than any confession, because a confession can be a lie and a silence in your presence can't be."

It was the most precise description of the field anyone had given him — more precise than Crane's, than Tamsin's, than his own — and it had been delivered by a woman who wanted to use it to take human beings apart. He had spent the year being told what he was by people who needed him to be a fire or a blessing or a story. Holm needed him to be a tool, and so Holm had bothered to learn what the tool actually did. There was a horror in being seen clearly for the first time by the person least entitled to see you.

"No," he said.

"I know," she said, and opened the folder.

---

She did not slide it across to him. She held it on her knee and read from it, in the same weather-report voice, and Daniel understood within the first sentence that the no he had said was a wall, and walls photograph badly, and that she had known this when she let him say it, had wanted it said, the way you want a man to draw the card you've forced.

"Halcombe Aerospace," she read. "Your firm held the account from a date I have here to a date I have here. Reputation work, the filing says. Crisis communications." She turned a page. "There was an engineer. A safety report. A regional fleet, a certification, a part." She did not look up. "There was, some months on, a flight."

The stone went down into the deep place and this time Daniel heard it land.

"I'm not an aviation investigator, Mr. Mercer. I'm aware I'm in the room — it's a novel experience, choosing every word for whether I can stand behind it. So I'll say only what I can stand behind." She turned another page. "There is a body of material — emails, a deck, a strategy document with a footnote, a junior associate's contemporaneous notes that someone failed to destroy — describing a coordinated effort to discredit a witness named Priya Raman. The effort drew on her medical history. Her therapy records, specifically. Which are protected. Which were obtained, and used, by parties acting for your firm." She closed the folder, finally, and looked at him. "I'm not making a claim about the crash. I'm not qualified to, and I won't pretend to be. I'm making a claim about the records. That's a federal matter. That one I can stand behind."

Daniel sat very still. He was aware of his own pulse and aware that she was aware of it, and he reached, the way he had reached his whole life, for the thing that would make this a different story — not a lie, he no longer had lies, but a reframe, the grain of the wood, the angle of cut. He found, to his interest, that the reframe was intact. It was undamaged. It cost him nothing to hold.

"The Raman matter," he said, "was a personnel dispute. The woman was unwell. Her own employer's medical record establishes it. We didn't invent her instability; we found it, and we cited it, the way any party in a dispute cites the record. As for the crash —" and here he felt nothing, no foil, no flashbulb, no sting at all, because he believed it, had examined it and believed it, the belief sitting in him as solid and painless as his own name — "the crash was unrelated. The part passed certification. A disturbed person predicts ten disasters and gets credit for the one that happens. I told a frightened junior that two years ago and it was true then and it's true now."

And it didn't hurt. That was the thing. He said *the crash was unrelated* in a room that punished known falsehood, said it inside his own field, the field that had broken war-rooms and freed an innocent man and made a dinner party into a slaughter, and the field let it pass like a sincere prayer, because to the only court that the field recognized — the court of what the speaker knew, in the instant, to be true — Daniel was innocent. He watched Holm watch him say it. He watched her watch him not flinch.

"You believe that," she said. It was not a question, and there was something in her voice he had not heard from her yet, something almost like grief, the grief of a technician whose instrument has just returned a reading she cannot use. "I came in here half-expecting you to bleed. I had a man outside trained to watch for it. And you said *the crash was unrelated* in your own field and you didn't so much as blink." She studied him. "Do you know what that means, Mr. Mercer? It means your own curse can't touch the thing you've buried. The most honest room in the country, and you're the one liar in it who walks free, because you've done to yourself what the preacher's people do in tents. You've believed your way out of the only sin that mattered." She stood, smoothing her coat, the coat she had declined a place to put. "I find that more frightening than anything Crane's built. He teaches it to crowds. You taught it to yourself for free."

---

He should have ended it there. He had ended harder meetings with less. But she was at the door now, her hand on it, and she said the thing she had come the four floors to say, in the same flat voice, which was how he knew she meant every word and would feel no pain saying any of it.

"The records matter is real and it doesn't depend on the crash. I want to be clear with you, room or no room, because I'd rather you came for the right reason than the wrong one. You and Mr. Vance and two others are exposed on the Raman material — illegally obtained protected medical records, a federal hook, conspiracy if anyone wanted to build it, and I can want it built or not want it built, that's a discretion that lives in my office." She let it sit, the way she let things sit. "I'd rather not. I'd much rather have a colleague than a defendant. A colleague keeps his hours, his family, his name out of a filing that would end what's left of it — and end Mr. Vance, who I gather taught you the trade, who is seventy-one and would not enjoy the interior of a courtroom. A defendant gets a calendar he doesn't control and lawyers who tell him to stay out of rooms where he can't lie." She smiled, and it almost reached somewhere this time, almost. "You'd be very bad at being a defendant, Mr. Mercer. You'd be the only one in the building who couldn't take the stand."

"You're threatening my family," Daniel said.

"No." And it cost her nothing, the no; she stood in his field and said it and her face was as smooth as any tent's. "I'm threatening *you.* Your daughter is of course safe — of course she is, why would you even — your wife, your house, all of it, safe, that's not the lever and I'd be insulted if you thought it was. A defendant's family lives the defense with him, that's just arithmetic. I didn't put it in your house. You did, two years ago, when you took Halcombe's money. I'm only the person who read the file." She opened the door. "I'm not a cruel woman, and you'll want to make me a villain because it would be cleaner for you, and you of all men can't afford an untrue thing in your own head — you've got so few rooms left where it doesn't show. I love this country. I've buried people for it. I think a man who can make any liar bleed is too useful to leave on a roof watching the news. That's not villainy. That's worse. It's a reason." She stepped into the hall, into the soft edge of him and then past it, out of the field, where the next thing she said cost nothing and proved nothing and he had to take on faith, which was its own small joke. "Think about it. There's a flight Thursday. You'd be back by the weekend. We'd call it voluntary, because it would be — you'd have chosen it, the way you choose to keep your name off a filing. The best arrangements always are. You taught half of Washington that."

---

He did not decide that night. He told himself he did not decide that night, and the telling cost him nothing, because by some measure it was true; the decision had been made years ago in a side room with champagne in heavy tumblers, and everything since was only the bill arriving by post.

He sat in the kitchen after she'd gone, in the chair June used for homework, still warm, and he looked at the refrigerator and the drawing that was no longer on it and the place on the counter where, in another life, a child had written WE ARE A HAPPY FAMILY and then nearly unwritten it. Nadia found him there at some hour. She had stopped asking him how things were, because he could no longer give her the merciful nothing the question was built to receive, and so she asked, instead, the only kind of question left between them, which was the true kind.

"Who was the woman in the car," she said. She had seen it from the bedroom, the government plates, the man who waited and watched the window.

"Federal," Daniel said. "She wants me to stand in rooms while they question people. So the people can't lie." He turned the salt cellar a quarter-turn, the way he turned objects now when his hands needed somewhere to put the part of him that used to talk. "She has the Halcombe file. The records we pulled on Raman. It's real. If I don't go I'm a defendant, and so is Gideon, and it lives in the house for years."

Nadia did not say *it'll be okay.* She had not been able to say it for a year, and he watched the old reflex rise now and find no road and go back down, and the not-saying was the tenderest thing left in the marriage, the place where the white lie had been amputated and the limb still ached. She said, instead, the thing she could stand behind, the way Holm had, the way they all did now.

"You're going to go," she said.

"I don't — " Daniel began, and there it was, the foil, the flashbulb, the small bright sting behind the eyes, because the sentence he had started was *I don't know yet,* and he did know, had known since the door closed, and the field caught the front edge of the lie the instant it left him and charged him for it, cheap, a paper cut on the soft palate, the price of the smallest cowardice. He swallowed the rest of it. "Yes," he said, and the yes was true and cost him nothing. He had wanted, for half a second, to be a man who hadn't decided. The room would not let him say even that.

"For us," Nadia said. "Or for you."

He considered the question with the gravity it deserved, because it was the only question. *For us* would have been a kindness and a reframe, the spill made into a community. *For me* would have been the cold accurate thing. And the actual truth was worse than either, and he gave it to her because there was nowhere left to put it.

"For the file," he said. "I'm going to stand in their rooms so a thing I did to a woman two years ago stays out of a filing. That's the whole of it. There's no us in it and no country in it. I'm protecting the version of me that didn't do it." He heard it land flat in the kitchen, no flourish, no seed buried in anyone's pocket, just the fact, his old enemy, sitting on the counter where the drawing used to be. "She said I taught myself the loophole for free. She's the first person who's looked at me in a year and seen the actual thing instead of the fire or the blessing." He looked up at his wife. "And the actual thing goes to a black site on Thursday to keep his own lie buried. That's the man."

Nadia stood in the doorway where June used to stand, and she did not come to him and she did not leave, and she did the thing that was all they had now in place of comfort, which was to stay in the room and let it be true.

---

The flight was Thursday, as promised. They sent the same plain car, the same man drove, and Daniel sat in the back with a single bag and his two phones — the personal one and the one for clients who no longer called — and watched the city go by behind glass, the brownstones and the harbor cranes and the glass towers where men were, at that very moment, lying warmly and painlessly into telephones, safe, distant, free, the whole humming mediated country going about the comfortable business he was being flown three time zones to interrupt for the unlucky few who'd be brought within ten meters of him in rooms with no windows.

The site was unmarked, which he had expected. What he had not expected was how ordinary it was, a low building the color of nothing behind a fence the color of nothing, off a road the maps gave no name. He had imagined a threshold he could be photographed crossing — a seal, a sign, a checkpoint, a man with a gun who would say *welcome* or *halt.* There was none. There was a steel door and a keypad and an attendant who did not say his own name or ask for Daniel's, and the door opened on a sealed flat hum, and Daniel understood that this was the photograph, this exact nothing, that the absence of a threshold was the threshold, that the great machines of harm did not announce themselves because announcing was for governors and tents and men with something to sell.

He stood at the door a moment in the desert light. Behind him the car was already turning, unhurried, the driver's job done. Ahead the cold air came out at him from the building's lungs, climate-sealed, fluorescent, patient, the temperature of a place that did not care what season it was anywhere on earth. Somewhere inside it, behind another unmarked door, a man Daniel had not yet met was sitting in a room, not knowing that the worst thing about to happen to him was that, very soon, he would not be able to lie.

He had asked, on the plane, what he would be required to do. Holm's man had told him, pleasantly, that he wouldn't be required to do anything at all, and had gone back to his crossword, and Daniel had turned the answer over for three time zones and could find no floor under it, the way he could find no floor under so much now. He crossed the unmarked threshold into the humming cold, and the door sealed behind him with the soft completed sound of a thing that had been waiting, and the field went with him the way it always did, the way it always would, into the rooms.
