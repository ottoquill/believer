## Aftermath

The lawyer came on the fourth day with a legal pad and the particular gentleness of a man billing for the visit, and explained to Daniel, in the careful verbs of his trade, what a confession was worth.

It was worth a great deal, it turned out, and almost nothing, depending on who was buying. The clip had gone everywhere—Daniel on the stage, the blood at his collar, the falsified test and the eleven and the woman he had called unstable until the word stuck and the part failed and the plane came down—and the world had watched it forty million times and metabolized it the way the world metabolized everything now, completely and without consequence. A confession on a screen was content. It trended and untrended, and somewhere in it a U.S. attorney's office in the interior opened a file, and somewhere else a class of plaintiffs' counsel for the families of eleven dead opened a much thicker one, and Daniel lay in the bed and let the lawyer tell him, in straw-packed phrases, that he was going to lose everything he had not already lost.

"The good news," the lawyer said, which was how men like him began the bad, "is that nothing you said on that stage can be used against you directly. Compelled testimony. There's an argument—it's a novel argument, but it's a real one—that a man in your, ah, condition can't give a voluntary statement. That everything you say is coerced by definition." He clicked the pen. "We'd be arguing you're not competent to confess. That the curse confessed for you."

"It didn't," Daniel said.

"I'm aware of your position. I'm telling you the position that keeps you out of a cell."

"It didn't confess for me. It couldn't. That was the entire point of doing it." He felt the metal come up faintly under his tongue, the body's new reflex, taxing him now even for the lies he merely declined to tell. "If you build me a defense that says the curse made me do it, you've unsaid the one true thing I've ever done. I'd rather have the cell."

The lawyer wrote *client declines* on the pad in a hand that suggested he had written it before, about other men, and been right to.

---

They let him out on the seventh day with a list of medications and a brochure about stroke recovery that had a photograph of an older couple laughing on a dock, which Daniel kept, for a while, on the kitchen counter, because it was the most efficient lie in the apartment and he had come to admire efficiency wherever he found it.

The apartment was his now in the way a thing is yours when no one else wants it. Nadia had taken June and a third of the furniture to a smaller place across the park, near the hospital, near the colleague—Daniel had stopped pretending he didn't know the building—and what was left had the cleared, echoing quality of a stage after the set comes down. He moved through the high glass rooms and found that the field came with him, of course, the way it always had, so that the few people who entered—the cleaner, a nurse, the man from the management company about the lease he could no longer afford—drifted to the windows while they talked, gravitating to the soft edge of the radius without knowing they did it, leaving Daniel alone at the empty center of every room he stood in. He had read once, in a brochure of a different kind, that this was the loneliest feature of the condition. He thought now that the loneliest feature was that he no longer minded.

His face had not come all the way back. The left side ran a half-second behind the right, so that his smile arrived in two installments, and he had developed a way of speaking that economized on the muscles that no longer fully reported for work. People found it grave. People had always found him grave; the difference was that now it was true.

He was not charged that month, or the next. The machinery of consequence, he discovered, moved at the speed of paper and not at the speed of feeling, and so the worst thing he had ever done arrived not as a reckoning but as a docket, a series of dates on which men in good suits would meet in rooms and argue, very slowly, over a man's whole life as though it were a parcel of disputed land. Halcombe's lawyers filed to have the confession excluded. The families' lawyers filed to have it admitted. Both sides, Daniel noticed, agreed on exactly one thing, which was that what he had said in front of the cameras was not, by itself, worth anything; it had to be proven all over again in a room, with documents, the slow way, the only way that had ever counted. He had given them the truth and they had thanked him and gone looking for the evidence.

---

Tamsin came in the second week with a recorder she did not turn on, which was how he knew she had come as a friend and not as the thing she also was.

"They're saying you're a saint now," she said. She had grown thinner over the four years, the way people in her trade thinned, burning the story for fuel. "Half of them. The other half are saying you're a murderer who finally cracked. And the third half—I know that's too many halves—the third half are saying the whole thing was staged. That you can't really feel it. That the blood was a trick." She sat on the arm of the one good chair, at the edge, near the glass, and did not seem to notice she had chosen the edge. "There's a thread. Forty thousand posts. They've decided the confession proves you were always faking, because a real curse would've forced it out years ago, and since it didn't, the curse must be fake, so the confession must be theater. The logic eats itself. But it eats itself in a direction they find comfortable, so."

"So it stands."

"So it stands." She looked at him. "How are you. And don't give me a true one if it's going to cost you. I can read the room. I always could; you taught me that, watching you work, four years ago. I just didn't know I was learning it on you."

He considered the question, which was harder than it sounded, because *I'm fine* would sting and *I'm dying* would not, the body being a poor judge of magnitude when it came to him. "I'm ruined," he said, "and I'm not sorry, and those two things sit next to each other and don't touch, and I keep waiting for one of them to win and neither one does." It cost him nothing. It was the new shape of everything: accurate, unrelieved, free.

"That's the whole world now," Tamsin said. "You should see it from where I sit. We thought—God, we actually thought, in the beginning—that if you couldn't lie, the lies would stop. *The Man Who Cannot Lie.* I wrote that. I believed it. And instead the lies just moved. They went where you couldn't reach, onto the screens, into the crowds, into people's own heads, where they could believe them sincerely and feel no pain. You made truth a thing that only happens in rooms, between bodies, and the rooms kept getting smaller and the screens kept getting bigger, and now there's more truth in the world than there's ever been and it changes nothing, because nobody has to be in the room."

"You have a piece in that."

"I have a piece in that. *Truth and the Republic of Screens.* My editor wants a book." She stood. "I'm going to write it. I'm going to make it true and careful and it's going to be very good and it's going to change nothing, and I'm going to do it anyway, because the alternative is to stop, and I won't stop. That's my whole religion, if you want to know. I keep telling it right even though telling it right has stopped mattering. You'd think that would feel like despair." She picked up the recorder she hadn't used. "It feels like the only clean thing left. I don't know what that says about me."

"It says you're a doubter," Daniel said. "It's a slur now. You wear it well."

She almost smiled, the way Marcus almost smiled, the way everyone he loved had started almost-smiling, as though the full one had been rationed along with everything else.

---

Crane did not die, and did not stop, and that was the part the panels could not script, because they had built the whole season around a duel and a duel was supposed to have an end.

By the next morning the prophet had it framed, the way Daniel himself might have framed it, in his prime, with a master's economy. The Believer's confession was not a refutation of the Conviction. It was its proof. *Behold a man so eaten by doubt that he tore his own brain apart to be rid of it. Behold what the unbelieving life costs. He suffered because he doubted; we are spared because we believe; you have just watched, on your screens, doubt leave a body in front of forty million witnesses.* He wept for Daniel from the stage the following week. He prayed for him by name. The faithful wept with him. The movement did not shrink. It grew.

Daniel watched the sermon to the end, the metal rising once on its own, his body taxing him for some lie he hadn't known he was declining, and understood that he had not beaten the man and never would. You could wound a sincere man—he had, for half a second, on the stage; he had seen the flicker and so had the few hundred in the radius and so, by now, had everyone, the clip parsed frame by frame for the thing the doctrine had no room for. But you could not destroy him. There was nothing false in him to expose. The serenity was real, the comfort was real, the relief he sold to frightened people was real, and the relief was the product, and the product worked. The Convicted would outlive their prophet's flinch. They had stood in the field and lied and felt no pain, and no confession, no blood, no eleven names could compete with the lived fact of having once been afraid and then, by believing, not.

He had given the world a demonstration of what truth cost. Crane had repackaged it, that same week, as a demonstration of what doubt cost. The same image, read two ways, and the screen did not adjudicate between them. The screen never had.

---

Holm sent no one, which was its own message, and then in the third month sent a single line through the lawyer, unsigned, which the lawyer read aloud with the discomfort of a man relaying something he did not understand: *The program continues. Your participation is no longer required or possible. We have moved on to other instruments.*

Daniel understood it better than the lawyer did. *Other instruments.* They had decided that a man who could break by being used was too fragile an asset, and had gone looking for what they had meant to find all along, which was a way to have the field without the man. He thought of Salt then, inevitably—Victor Salt and his Halcyon and his synthetic conviction, belief-on-demand—and he laughed, once, the half-second-late laugh, alone in the empty apartment, because of course the two of them would meet in the middle. Holm wanted a field with no Daniel. Salt wanted a Crane you could install. The state wanted compelled truth and the market wanted painless belief, and they were building it from opposite ends: a world where you could be made unable to lie and made able to believe anything at once, so that the unable-to-lie would no longer matter, because nothing would be left that anyone knew to be false.

The brochure on Halcyon's pilot had shipped that month. *Synthetic Conviction: clinical-grade certainty for an uncertain age.* Daniel read the copy with a professional's eye and found it flawless. He could not have written it better. It promised, in the present tense, the one thing the catastrophe had taught the species to crave: not truth, which hurt, and not lies, which now hurt too, but the third thing—the painless interior, the conscience that has died in peace.

It would sell. He had spent his life selling the artisanal version, one governor at a time, and here was the scaled product, and the only difference was that his version had required him to be in the room.

---

The marriage did not end. It had ended already, weeks before, in a hospital chair, level and without raised voices, the way the worst things in their house had always ended. What happened now was the part that came after the ending, which no one ever named because there was no drama in it—the settling, the long cooling, the negotiation of a peace between two people who had stopped being at war and had not become anything else.

They met, by arrangement, in the park between the two apartments, where the field was a non-issue because there was nothing left either of them needed to lie about. June ran ahead toward the carousel and Daniel and Nadia walked behind at the pace of people who had once walked everywhere together and now measured the distance.

"The lawyer says you might do time," Nadia said. She said it the way she said the other unbearable things, level, her doctor's voice, the one that had told a hundred parents the worst news in the kindest available true words. "I want June ready, if it happens. I don't want to lie to her about where you are. I can't anyway. So I want to tell her the true version, and I want it to be the version that doesn't break her, and I need you to help me find which true thing that is. You're good at that. You were always good at that."

"That's the first time you've asked me for that since it started."

"It's the first time the truth's been the only tool that works." She watched June reach the carousel and stop, deciding, the way she decided everything now, with an eleven-year-old's terrible care. "I spent the whole marriage resenting that you could make any true thing sound like a different true thing. And now my daughter's father might go to prison and I find I want the one thing you can still do. There's a word for that. I don't want to say it in front of the field."

"Hypocrisy's safe," Daniel said. "It's an opinion. It won't cost you."

"It's not hypocrisy." She stopped walking. "It's that I finally understand what you did for a living. You found, in a bad situation, the true sentence a person could carry out of the room and still stand up. That was your whole gift. You used it to ruin people. But it was the same gift, Daniel. It was always the same gift. I do it with the parents every day. You did it with governors. The only difference was the direction you pointed it." She started walking again. "Point it at our daughter for once. That's all I'm asking. Find me the true thing she can carry."

He thought about it the rest of the walk, the way he had once thought about a chyron, and the thinking did not hurt, because finding the kindest accurate sentence had never been a lie—only a choice about which truths to set in the front of the arrangement and which to leave, real and untouched, in the back.

"Tell her," he said, "that I did a wrong thing a long time ago, and that I told the truth about it on purpose, even though no one could make me, and that telling it is why I might have to go away for a while. That it's not a thing that happened to me. It's a thing I chose. Children can carry *chose*. It's *happened-to* that breaks them." The metal did not rise. It was all true. "And if she asks whether you'll tell her the truth from now on, tell her yes, and mean it, because in this family it's the only currency that didn't get devalued."

Nadia was quiet for a moment. "That's good," she said. "That's annoyingly good." And then, lower, not for the field, just for him: "I did love you. I want to say it where I can't take it back. I stopped, and I'm not going to start again, and none of that touches the part that was true. We were the same crime in different keys, you said that once. I think we were also, for a while, a real thing. I don't need you to agree. I just needed to say it somewhere it would cost me if it weren't true."

It had cost her nothing. He watched it cost her nothing, and understood that this was the only proof either of them would ever have, or need, and that it was, in its way, the most honest wedding vow of their entire marriage, spoken at the end of it, in a park, with the field bearing witness like a notary who could not be bribed.

"It was real," Daniel said. The metal stayed down. "For a while. It was real."

They stood at the edge of the carousel's painted weather, and June came around once on a horse the color of nothing, waving, certain, and went around again into the part of the turn where they could not see her, and came back, and waved again, and the two of them stood there being her parents in the new and separate way, neither reconciled nor at war, two honest people who had stopped flaying each other because they had stopped needing to be one thing. The carousel turned. The city did its grey work behind them, the cranes lifting and setting down. Somewhere men in good suits were arguing slowly over the rest of his life, on a schedule that had nothing to do with the child going round in front of him, and he let them, because there was nothing in the world he could arrange about it now, and the not-arranging was its own strange rest.

June came around again and waved, and they raised their hands, and neither of them said it was going to be okay.
